Consumer sentiment is at 78.2, the lowest since late 2022 and a record low among mortgage holders. The Westpac "time to buy a dwelling" index is 89.6, against a long run average of 112.6. The cash rate has just moved to 4.85%.

Many investors who are financially prepared are holding off until conditions feel safer. Our new report, **The Cost of Waiting: New Property Edition**, looks at about 25 years of Australian data to test whether that has paid off in the past, and what it means under today's settings.

Here's what the research shows.

**1. Building costs have not gone backwards**

The ABS price index for new homes (building only, weighted average of the eight capital cities) tells a consistent story:

* Since 2000, it never fell by more than 1% over any 6, 12, 24 or 36 month period
* No single quarter recorded a fall larger than 0.5%
* Over 6 months, it rose by more than 1% in 72% of periods, with a median rise of 1.8% every six months
* Over 12 months, it rose by more than 1% in 91% of periods (median +3.2%). Over 24 months, 99% (median +6.6%). Over 36 months, 100% (median +10.9%)

And the rises have been getting bigger. Build prices rose 4.7% to 5.3% in the year to September 2026, and prices builders received rose 5.7%. During the 2022 to 2023 rate rises, while established home prices fell, build prices rose 6.7%.

Build prices are now about 46% higher than in mid 2021, and 186% higher than in 2000.

On a $650,000 package with a 60% build share, the median build price rise alone added about $6,800 after 6 months, $12,600 after 12 months and $25,700 after 24 months.

Why? New homes are priced on land, labour, materials and finance. Labour shortages, builder insolvencies and planning constraints all limit how far builders can cut. In quiet markets, they tend to hold prices and offer incentives rather than discounts.

**2. Land has risen even faster over time**

Land is the bigger driver of long term value. Since 2000, national land value per person is up more than 500%.

* National land values rose by more than 1% in 77% of years, with a median rise of 7.6% a year
* Over 36 months, they rose in 96% of periods, with a median rise of 24%
* Every annual fall since 2000 was followed by a rise within one to two years

The median lot price shows the trend clearly: about $185,000 in December 2009, $280,000 in September 2018, $340,000 in September 2023 and $403,570 in the March quarter 2026, up 8.7% on a year earlier.

From March 2022 to December 2025, median lot prices in Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth rose 62% to 71%. Population growth of about 1.4% a year and slow lot release continue to support land prices over time.

Land can fall in the short term, though. National land values fell in 2009, 2011, 2012, 2019 and 2023, and Victorian growth corridors with heavy lot supply saw falls in 2019 and 2023 to 2024. That's why location matters more than timing.

**3. Confidence has tended to recover after prices, not before**

When consumer confidence was below its long run average, build prices were more than 1% higher a year later in 97% of months. In every completed low confidence episode since 2008, established prices hit their low before confidence recovered, and build prices rose while buyers waited.

Headlines such as "prices fall 7%" usually describe established homes. For investors in new property, that distinction matters.

**4. What waiting means for an investor today**

The report models a $650,000 house and land package: 40% land, a 12 month build, an 80% interest only loan at 7.6%, rent at 3.9% of value, a 39% tax rate, new build depreciation and the 50% CGT discount, measured after tax over 10 years.

In year one of the completed home, the after tax cash flow is about−$7,500 (rent $24,300, less interest $39,500 and costs $5,800, plus a tax benefit of $13,500 including $13,700 depreciation). Interest during construction is not deductible.

An investor who waits avoids that carrying cost but gives up growth during the wait:

* Prices flat: the waiter is about $17,000 ahead after 12 months
* Prices rise 3.5% a year: about $11,000 behind
* Prices rise 6% a year: about $30,000 behind after 12 months, $61,000 after 24

The break even is about 2.2% a year of price growth during the wait. Historical median rises of 3.2% for the build and 5.7% for the 60/40 package over 12 months sat above that more often than not. The main exceptions were the land falls of 2019 and 2023.

**5. Risks that favour careful selection**

* Off the plan apartments in high supply areas. In 2018 to 2019, about 60% in Sydney and 53% in Melbourne were valued below contract at settlement.
* Land heavy packages in corridors with heavy lot supply
* Builder financial weakness. In 2025 to 2026, 712 residential builders entered external administration.
* Escalation (rise and fall) clauses in building contracts

Construction delays also cost investors. An extra six months of build delay costs about $15,000 to $20,000 on a $650,000 package.

**6. Factors that favour acting earlier**

* **Fixed price building contracts**

   lock in today's base build price while construction costs keep rising
* **Tax settings favour new builds.**

   From 1 July 2027, new builds keep negative gearing deductions against other income, while established homes bought after 12 May 2026 do not. New dwellings may also choose the 50% CGT discount or indexation.
* **The pending "new residential dwelling" cut off.**

   A bill before the Senate, not yet law, would cover homes bought within 24 months of a first occupancy certificate issued after 12 May 2026. Older completed stock may not qualify.
* **Time limited incentives**

   have often been wound back as markets recover, though not always

**The bottom line**

Waiting for confidence has rarely made the building cheaper, and over time land has risen faster still. Whether waiting costs more overall depends on land in the specific area, the incentives available, and the project and builder. An investor who is financially prepared and plans to hold for the long term is less exposed to timing.

**Questions investors should ask**

* Am I waiting for a specific financial reason, or because the news about established homes feels bad?
* Could I manage repayments 2 percentage points higher, including non deductible interest during construction?
* What is happening to land prices and lot supply in the area I'm considering?
* Does the contract have a fixed price, and what does it exclude? Is there an escalation clause?
* Will the property meet the "new residential dwelling" tests, and what is my after tax cash flow?
* Have I checked the builder: their license, finances and insurance?
